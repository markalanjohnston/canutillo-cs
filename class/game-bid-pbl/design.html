The cardboard is a costume. Games have jumped technologies since the 1840s — hand-painted boards, printing presses, plastic, and now your pocket. What jumps isn’t the board; it’s the core loop: what players do, choose, and win. This week you find your game’s body, then dress it for the phone. Lo que salta de tecnología no es el tablero — es el ciclo central: hacer, decidir, ganar.

Step 1 — Map the core loop (with your crew)

Five boxes, agreed as a crew, on the template in Google Classroom. Your own Field Survey answers are the source — you already wrote what a player does on a turn.

BoxWhat goes in it
1 · Setup · preparaciónWhat’s ready when a round begins.
2 · The turn · el turnoWhat a player DOES — verbs, not pieces: roll, choose, move.
3 · The choice · la decisiónThe decision a player controls — where skill lives.
4 · Resolution · resoluciónWhat changed after the turn.
5 · Win · victoriaThe condition — and how the game announces it.

Step 2 — The jump: survives, changes form, or dies

Rule on each element of YOUR game:

  1. SURVIVES — works the same on a phone. (Yahtzee’s five dice — the client kept them.)
  2. CHANGES FORM — same job, new shape. (Trouble’s popper → a tap with a spin animation.)
  3. DIES — the phone doesn’t need it. (The rulebook. The lost piece under the couch.)

Then finish, in your own words: “The choice in our game lives in ___ because ___.” That line becomes your Play screen’s job. La decisión de tu juego será el trabajo de tu pantalla de juego.

Step 3 — Wireframe Screen 1 (on paper, on purpose)

A wireframe is a screen’s skeleton: boxes and labels, drawn fast, judged on clarity. The rules:

  1. Boxes and labels ONLY. No art — ugly on purpose. Pretty comes later, in Slides.
  2. Mark every tappable thing, with its label. If it can be touched, it says so.
  3. One screen, one job — written under the frame.
  4. The map feeds the screens: your TURN box is your Play screen; your WIN box is your end screen.

Cajas y etiquetas solamente; marca todo lo que se toca; una pantalla, un trabajo.

Step 4 — The cold-read (how you know it works)

Trade frames with a partner. The reader says what they think each element does. The designer stays SILENT. If the reader gets it, they initial the corner. If they need you to explain, the screen failed — fix the labels, not the partner. This is criterion 2 (“walkable without your crew explaining it”) rehearsed on paper.

Then photograph your frame and attach it in Google Classroom (check it isn’t blurry before you submit).
