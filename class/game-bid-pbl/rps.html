Three languages, three jobs

Every page you have ever opened is these three, working together. You have already met the first one in the editor.

LanguageIts one jobIn this game
HTML · estructuraThe structure — what things ARE. A count, two player boxes, a result line, a button. Boxes and labels, typed out.
CSS · estiloThe look — colors, spacing, shape. Navy and orange, rounded corners, the two boxes side by side.
JavaScript · comportamientoThe behavior — what HAPPENS. Counting down, listening for keys, deciding who won.

Notice the order that mirrors your own project: structure first, looks second, behavior last. That is why the client made you draw boxes and labels before anything got pretty — and why nobody writes code before the wireframe is right.

How one round actually works

  1. Start is clicked. Both throws are erased, the old timers are cancelled, and the count begins.
  2. The count runs — the 3 appears instantly, then three timers fire the rest, 0.7 seconds apart.
  3. THROW! opens the window. Only now does the game start listening. Press early and nothing happens — same as throwing before "shoot" in real life.
  4. A key is pressed. The game checks: is it one of the six keys? Whose is it? Has that player already thrown? Your first press locks in — no changing your mind.
  5. The screen says READY, not what you threw. That is the whole trick: your opponent learns that you went, never what you picked.
  6. Both in → reveal. Compare the two throws, name the winner.
  7. One second after THROW! the window shuts. A missing throw is a BAD THROW and the round is replayed — nobody wins on a technicality.

Remember ruling that the rulebook DIES on the jump to a phone? It did not disappear — it moved into the code, and it fits on one line: { rock: "scissors", paper: "rock", scissors: "paper" }, each throw paired with the throw it beats. If Player 1 threw rock and that line says rock beats scissors, Player 1 wins when Player 2 threw scissors. Three rules, one line, no tangle of if-statements. El reglamento no desapareció: se mudó al código, y cabe en una línea.

Three screens on paper — one screen here. Why?

The wireframes for this game were three screens: Start, Play, Result. The thing you just played is one screen that changes what it shows. So was the paper wrong?

No — the paper did its job. Drawing three screens forced the question "what is each one FOR?", and the answers survived: get two players in, take the throw, name the winner. When it came time to build, those three jobs fit on one screen with a count box and a result line, so keeping them apart would have meant three page-loads for a five-second game. That decision is design work, and you only get to make it because the paper made the jobs clear first.

Your bid is different on purpose: the client asked for three to five screens in Slides, and the fence is the fence. Build what the client asked for — then be ready to say what you would change if you were building it for real. El papel no se equivocó: hizo visible el trabajo de cada pantalla.

What is deliberately missing — and why that is the point

No name entry. No best-of-three. No score history. No sound. No animations. Those all sound good, and every one of them was cut on purpose.

This is an MVP — a minimum viable product: the smallest version that genuinely works. It does one thing, completely. You build that first, you find out whether the idea is any good, and then you add. A version with a title screen, a scoreboard, and no working round is worth nothing.

It is not even cheat-proof: nothing stops Player 1 from reaching over and pressing Player 2's keys. An MVP trusts its two players. How would you fix that? Answer honestly and you have just written your first feature request — which is exactly what a client does to you. Un MVP hace UNA cosa, completa. Lo demás se corta a propósito.
