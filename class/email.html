The Professional Email ✉️

Mr. Johnston · Canutillo High School · a skill you’ll still be using at 25 — and 55

Every email you send is read by a person who is deciding, in about five seconds, whether to answer you. Teachers, coaches, colleges, recruiters, managers — for most of the adults who will control doors in your life, email is the front door. This page is how you knock so the door opens. Cada correo lo lee una persona que decide, en unos cinco segundos, si te va a responder. Esta página te enseña a tocar la puerta para que se abra.

What’s the big deal?

A professional email isn’t fancy. It’s five small marks of respect for the reader’s time, and you can hit all five in under three minutes. In my classes, real emails earn a score out of five — one point per mark — because I want checking them to become automatic before anything important is riding on it.

The five marks

1 · Subject · asunto
Your words, saying what the email is about. (In school: start with your class and period.) The subject is how busy people sort — blank or vague means unread.
2 · Body · cuerpo
A greeting with the person’s name, who you are, and ONE clear ask. One email, one question.
3 · Signature · firma
A closing (“Thank you,”) and your full name. Every time.
4 · Grammar · gramática
Capitals, punctuation, complete sentences. Email is not texting.
5 · Formatting · formato
Line breaks between greeting, body, and signature. No wall of text.

The same ask, two ways

Subject: (none)
hey its dani did u get my essay i turned it in
Subject: History 1301, 10:30 section — confirming my essay submission
Good afternoon Professor Reyes,

My name is Dani M. and I’m in your 10:30 section. The portal shows my Tuesday essay as missing, but I submitted it before the deadline. Could you check whether it came through on your side?

Thank you,
Dani M.
Same student, same problem. The first one gets deleted — or answered a week later with “who is this?” The second gets answered the same day, and the professor now knows Dani as someone careful. That reputation was free.

Here’s why it’s a big deal

Not just because it’s “the professional way to communicate.” That’s true and it’s the weakest reason on this page.

1 · You are often being interviewed and don’t know it — a true story

When I left the Air Force I was, honestly, a little listless — out of uniform and not sure what came next. At a job fair I had an informal interview for a customer-service job at a stock brokerage, and I was asked to email my résumé to a senior vice president. The SVP wrote back. I answered. Several emails went back and forth — questions, details, scheduling — before I ever got an interview.

At the interview they told me something I have never forgotten: the emails were the interview. They could teach the finance side to anyone, they said. What they couldn’t do was justify hiring someone who couldn’t communicate. Every message I’d sent had been quietly answering the only question that mattered.

That was one of many times professional communication has opened a door for me. It’s a big part of why this page exists. — Mr. Johnston

2 · Employers say it out loud, every year

LinkedIn’s most-in-demand-skills research for 2024 ranked communication #1 — first, ahead of every technical skill on the list. It’s also why university writing centers publish email guides for arriving freshmen, and why job sites keep “how to write a professional email” permanently on the shelf (all linked below). Nobody writes “sends clear email” on a résumé — but every employer finds out within the first week.

3 · Writing is proof

A conversation evaporates. An email is dated, written, and sitting in both inboxes. So professionals follow up conversations that matter with a short email: “Thanks for talking with me today. To make sure I have it right: …”

That one habit does two jobs at once. It catches misunderstandings today, while they’re cheap to fix — if you and the other person walked away with different versions of the agreement, the reply tells you now, not next month. And it becomes the record — proof of what was agreed, and proof that you did what you said you’d do. A teacher grants an extension, a manager approves a day off, a customer agrees to a price: get it into writing, kindly, every time. Una conversación se evapora; un correo es la prueba escrita, con fecha, en las dos bandejas de entrada.

4 · It’s the most democratic door in professional life

You can’t phone a CEO. You can’t drop by an admissions office in another state. But nearly everyone — recruiters, admissions officers, authors, founders — reads email, and a well-written message from a high-school student lands on the same screen as everyone else’s mail and gets judged only by what’s on it. Very few tools give you that kind of reach for free. The catch: it only works if the message survives those first five seconds.

5 · “But nobody emails anymore”

People said the same thing about the business letter. Then the skills moved — intact — from paper to email: put the point up front, make one clear ask, sign your name. If your future workplace runs on Slack or Microsoft Teams (many do), those same skills are already waiting for you there, and the person who writes clearly wins in every channel. The tools change; the skill compounds. Learn it once and carry it to whatever replaces email next.

The moves, all in one place

Before you hit send

  • Count the five marks: subject in your words · greeting, who you are, one ask · closing + full name · real sentences · line breaks.
  • Read it out loud once. Your ear catches what your eye skips.
  • To is the person you need action from; CC keeps someone in the loop; BCC hides a long address list from itself. Reply All only when every recipient truly needs your answer.
  • Stay in the thread. Same topic, same thread — a brand-new email with no history makes the reader rebuild the whole story.
  • Follow up conversations in writing. “To make sure I have it right:…” (see #3 above).
  • Your address is read before your message. A school address is professional by default; when you make a personal one, make it a version of your name.
  • Assume it gets forwarded. Never put in an email what you wouldn’t want a third person to read.

Where this follows you after graduation

  • College: professors, advisors, financial aid — office hours get scheduled, extensions get granted, and recommendation letters get requested by email.
  • Work: applications, recruiters, and your first impression on a manager you haven’t met yet.
  • The military: the services run on written communication; clear writing gets noticed early.
  • Adult life: landlords, insurance, billing errors — the paper trail is how problems actually get fixed.
